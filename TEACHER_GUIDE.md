# Teacher guide: Four Seasons to Grandma’s Table

**Audience:** high school. **Status:** playable classroom draft, with live dictionary verification completed and local cultural/classroom review pending. Use [source notes](SOURCE_NOTES.md) and [content review](CONTENT_REVIEW.md) when working with local American Indian Education and appropriate tribal language/education partners.

## Learning goals

- Distinguish a reservation boundary, parcel ownership, and a partial land-restoration remedy. Explain why a contemporary home does not erase community dispossession.
- Identify the date, author, purpose, and limits of historical evidence. Separate sourced community events from the fictional family’s household and ancestors.
- Use Ojibwe food words in meaningful requests and actions, including the distinction among **nibi** (water), **ziinzibaakwadwaaboo** (maple sap), and **zhiiwaagamizigan** (maple syrup). English remains available.
- Interpret a freezing-night/warmer-day pattern, observe a stability problem, follow a known grower’s marked-share plan, and compare earlier observations with contemporary measurements.
- Recognize shared food preparation and supervised fishing. The game does not teach plant identification, tree tapping, basket manufacture, rice-harvest methods, or ice assessment.
- Discuss fictional applications of Wisdom, Love, Respect, Bravery/Courage, Honesty, Humility, and Truth. Wording varies across communities; cultural authenticity and moral worth are not scored.

## History and fictional chronology

The opening records-room task is required. Players read three cards, compare them, and caption a family photo with a claim the evidence supports. The optional 2024 update extends the discussion.

| Date on a historical card | Evidence and purpose |
| --- | --- |
| 1855 | Reservation establishment by treaty, as described in a 1998 federal court opinion; the boundary illustration is not a real survey or parcel-title record. |
| 1889 | Nelson Act allotment and land sales, from that court’s historical account. |
| 1948–1959 | Sales without majority rightful-owner consent, and a 1959 order to stop, as recorded in Congress’s 2020 findings. |
| 2020 | Enacted law directing approximately 11,760 acres into trust for the Band: a partial remedy, not proof that every loss was repaired. |
| 2024 | Archived newspaper reporting on restoration and a tribal leader’s connection between dispossession and housing needs; not a directly consulted tribal release. |

The **played adventure** uses only 1926 and 2026. Nookomis lives in a fictional rented home near Cass Lake in 2026. Waaban, Niibin, Aki, and Aunt Biboon are relatives in that present. The kitchen photo introduces ancestors Waabigwan, Ishkode, and Giizhig in 1926. Spring’s split-pine crossing and fall’s dock crossing reach them; returning through those paths brings the player to 2026. Summer and winter stay in 2026. `TIMELINE` displays travel separately from the historical cards.

The sources establish community land loss. They do not establish an individual property history for this invented household. The story does not imply that all families were displaced in the same way, that reservation boundaries guarantee ownership of every parcel, or that making a meal repairs dispossession.

## Pacing and facilitation

The original design target was about **25 minutes**; allow approximately **28–30 minutes** for the revised records-room opening and seasonal adventure, plus discussion as needed. This is an estimate, not a timed student trial. Reading, exploration, paired play, and repeated conversations can extend it.

Encourage `EXAMINE` and `ASK`. A pair can share keyboard and evidence-note roles. `HINT` advances from a nudge to a clue to explicit commands for the current step. There is no countdown, death, or mistake penalty. Dropped items stay recoverable; unsuccessful commands keep essential objects.

Language is part of the task, not a spelling test: `ASK NIIBIN ABOUT MIINAN`, `LABEL SAP AS ZIINZIBAAKWADWAABOO`, and `GIVE MANOOMIN TO NOOKOMIS` work alongside English equivalents. Use `TRANSLATE`, word cards, and `JOURNAL` to check meanings. Seasonal words have their dictionary’s complete “it is…” meanings. Nookomis means “my grandmother,” the player’s relationship to June.

`HELP` lists commands; arrow keys recall them. `SAVE` creates a checkpoint distinct from autosave; `LOAD` restores it. `RESTART` asks before erasing both. Saves remain in that browser and website origin. Start a new game to experience the complete revised opening. Do not enter student names or identifying information.

## Three discussion questions

1. How can Nookomis have a home today while community land loss and housing needs remain part of the story? Which claims could the photo, map, and law support—and which could they not support?
2. How did the author and date of a source matter? Compare the federal accounts, reported tribal leader’s perspective, and the notebook/chart puzzle. What further community knowledge would you seek?
3. Where did an Ojibwe word, another person’s work, or a teaching change what you did? How could a mistaken first attempt be corrected without taking credit for everyone’s contribution?

Keep humor in family banter, kitchen mishaps, and navigation. Dispossession is not a joke or a problem solved by time travel. If students raise a local language, teaching, or historical difference, record it for review rather than treating the game as definitive.
