# Teacher guide: Four Seasons to Grandma’s Table

**Audience:** high school. **Status:** playable classroom draft; not culturally reviewed. Coordinate review with local American Indian Education and appropriate tribal language/education partners before classroom use. Vocabulary is now checked against published archival dictionary resources; English meanings and commands remain available. Live dictionary checking and local preferred forms still need review.

## Learning goals

- Interpret a night/day temperature pattern; distinguish sap collection from the time and shared work needed for syrup.
- Use observation and a simple materials repair rather than arbitrary guessing; follow a known grower’s marked harvest plan.
- Compare historical observations and contemporary measurements, and explain the limits of each source when considering water and habitat.
- Recognize collaboration in food preparation and in a supervised fishing plan. The game does not teach plant identification, tree tapping, rice-harvest practices, or ice assessment.
- Discuss grounded applications of Wisdom, Love, Respect, Bravery/Courage, Honesty, Humility, and Truth, recognizing variations across communities. No cultural authenticity or moral worth is scored.

## Pacing and facilitation

Target about **25 minutes**: 2–3 minutes at Grandma’s kitchen, roughly 4–5 per season, and 2–3 for returning to the table and reviewing the journal. This is a design target, not a timed student trial; fluent command users or readers following the walkthrough finish faster. Allow extra reading time or paired play as appropriate.

Encourage EXAMINE and ASK, not just rushing the objective. A pair can share keyboard and note-taking roles. HINT advances from a nudge to a clue to explicit commands, and resets for each puzzle step. There is no countdown, death, or penalty for mistakes. Dropped supplies stay in the location where they were left; failed commands explain the next useful action.

English commands work throughout. HELP lists commands; arrow keys recall history. SAVE creates a checkpoint distinct from autosave; LOAD returns to it. RESTART asks before erasing both. Saves remain only on that browser and origin; changing browsers or clearing site data removes access to them. Do not enter student names or identifying information.

## Three optional discussion questions

1. Where did an earlier observation help someone in the present, and where did a modern measurement help someone in the earlier setting? What could neither source prove?
2. Choose one teaching from your journal. Which action put it into practice, and how could a mistaken first attempt be repaired?
3. How did small contributions, environmental limits, and other people’s work change the meaning of the family meal?

Time travel and the tote’s preservation are explicit fictional devices, not Ojibwe beliefs. The historical scenes are fictional representations, not reconstructions of a particular community’s practices. If a student raises a local teaching or language difference, record it for the content review process instead of treating the game wording as definitive.
